## 1. The load-bearing correction: no spatial “where” before space

If space is emergent, the primitive generator cannot assume a pre-existing spatial coordinate system or a particular point in it. An initial condition could be one relational state, a generating constraint, a collection of starting relations, or an atemporal formal totality. A **causal source/root** in a process is not automatically a **spatial center** in an emergent three-dimensional space.

Use this separation:

- **Generative specification:** rule(s) or constraint(s) determining admissible transformations; not necessarily an event.
- **Causal origin:** a minimal element, initial state, or ancestry condition in an order of events; not necessarily a unique spatial location.
- **Space:** a derived network of relational proximity whose 3-dimensionality has to be demonstrated.
- **Time (explicitly defined by the researcher):** **the sequence of causal events**. It is *not* seconds, a clock parameter, a pre-existing physical duration, or a foliation to be chosen by an observer. A model may represent the causal sequence through a graph/order, but this is a modeling method rather than a replacement for the researcher's definition.
- **Matter:** a separately derived property of persistent/dynamically stable structure; no primitive matter-location variable has been supplied.

The researcher's proposed unit **causal step** is neither a distance divided by time nor Einstein's constant c. This inquiry must not take the word *speed* as automatic permission to import metric notions before they emerge.

## 3. “All at once” in the researcher's causal-event meaning of time

**Correction to the initial assistant framing:** The researcher explicitly defines **time as the sequence of causal events**. It is an error to recast the original question as though “time” meant an external parameter that must be eliminated, or to treat “simultaneous” automatically as a comparison of clock readings. In this inquiry, time is causal succession *by definition*; seconds, hours, synchronized clocks, and relativistic temporal coordinates are not its primitive meaning.

“One beginning” can mean one event/condition causally antecedent to the histories in question. “Many beginnings” can mean several minimal causal events or conditions. “All possibilities simultaneously” can mean that many possible causal sequences exist and do not have to be placed into a single shared total order. Causal independence is not identical to a measurable spatial or clock-time separation. Whether an event network is a total order, partial order, or another structure remains **unselected** and requires mathematical definition, not a conventional-time assumption.

An optional partial-order model (E,≺) can encode the claim that event a causally precedes event b. Incomparable events have no established precedence **under that relation**. They can be called “parallel” as an informal picture, but equality of an external timestamp or physically measured simultaneity does **not** follow. A chain count or causal-depth number may be a convenient discrete summary of succession; it is not yet seconds or a proof that causation is globally clocked.

**Consequence:** With time defined by causal succession, the alternatives “one beginning,” “many causal beginnings,” and “multiple coexisting causal histories” remain live candidate structures. There is no need to posit an external master time to discuss them; nor does the presence of a first causal event imply a privileged spatial location in a later emergent geometry.

## 4. What Wolfram's Ruliad does and does not assert

Stephen Wolfram's **Ruliad** (2021) is proposed as an entangled limiting object of **all possible computational rules, all initial conditions, and all ways of applying them**, including branching and merging histories. Its observed physics is tied in his proposal to the sampling/coarse-graining of particular observer classes, not automatically to an externally running infinite quantum experiment.

The Wolfram Physics Project distinguishes the *spatial hypergraph* (proposed to produce physical space), a *causal graph of update events*, and the *multiway/rulial graph* of alternative histories/rules. **A branch of computational possibility is not thereby a direction or position in our observed physical space.** Spatial distance and inter-history separation should not be conflated.

The researcher's intuitive proposal—only configurations sufficiently interactable/connected/persistent with an observer become visible—resembles Wolfram's bounded-observer motif, but “survival” needs an explicit criterion. Being causally inaccessible is not the same as ceasing to exist; different histories may merge, and the accessible substructure is a distinct selection object.

**Prior-art boundary:** The Wolfram/Ruliad approach is a speculative research program, not an empirically demonstrated all-possibilities quantum origin of the physical universe. Its own exposition states the mathematical construction remains technically incomplete.

## 5. A concrete non-circular test for emergent three-dimensionality

Let H be an **emergent spatial-proximity graph or hypergraph**, distinct from the causal event graph and from the multiway possibility graph. Suppose an intrinsic graph-path distance d_H(x,y) can be justified from observable relational interactions.

Define the ball count:

```
V_x(r) = number of physically accessible spatial nodes y with d_H(x,y) ≤ r.
```

For an effectively 3D Euclidean regime, one expects at appropriate scales:

```
V_x(r) ~ C_x r^3
d_eff(x,r) ≈ d log(V_x(r)) / d log(r) ≈ 3.
```

The exponent is scale-dependent, and finite graphs have boundary effects. This is an **emergent geometric criterion**, not a premise that every causal-step network is 3D. A tree with fixed branching b>1 has volume growth roughly b^r, **not** r^3; unlimited proliferation of possible paths by itself does not produce ordinary three-dimensional space.

Hence the researcher's proposal **needs some mechanism of relational constraint, joining, quotienting under stated physical equivalence, or selective persistence** to prevent generic history-branch growth from being mistaken for 3D adjacency. A physically justified quotient must not collapse observed differences merely to force an exponent of three.

A relational model must additionally explain why spatial geometry is approximately homogeneous/isotropic on large enough scales, why particle-like persistent patterns exist, and why an observer sees one coherent notion of space despite microscopic branching.

## 6. Is matter necessarily concentrated near a causal root?

**No.** That implication does not follow from the two premises.

The earliest *causal* state may not be an element of any later *spatial* slice; “distance from the causal origin” may only mean ancestry depth, not spatial radius. Even if a graph has one root vertex, that vertex is not necessarily a preferred coordinate in the emergent spatial geometry. Likewise, mass/matter has yet to be defined: in relational physics proposals, matter might be stable graph motifs, excitations, defects or conserved local structures appearing **throughout** the generated network.

If the construction retains a particular privileged vertex and the resulting emergent spatial metric makes this vertex a geometrical center, then a centered density profile **might** arise. But that requires additional assumptions about graph geometry, matter creation, transport and symmetry breaking. A central pile-up is **one possible prediction of particular models**, not a necessary result of causal ancestry.

Conventional homogeneous Big Bang cosmology also has no spatial explosion center. NASA's John Mather notes the distinction between a hot/dense past everywhere and a blast from one location:
https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/big-bang-q-and-a/
https://asd.gsfc.nasa.gov/archive/mather/faq/cosmology.html

A classical initial singularity may indicate limits of the geometric theory and should not be identified casually with a localized physical generator:
https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/spacetime-singularities/

## 7. Strongest defensible next research work

Hold the two original premises fixed and keep the four origin scenarios as **alternatives**. Before arguing from a beginning to observable matter concentration, separately specify:

1. what counts as one causal step without using spatial distance or clock duration;
2. whether the researcher's **causal-event sequence** is best represented as a total order, partial order, branching relation, or a family of possible event sequences;
3. what maps the causal relation to an emergent spatial adjacency relation (do not equate causal graph, branchial graph and physical-space graph);
4. which constraints on the network yield 3D ball growth and observer-stable structures;
5. what physical state observable corresponds to *matter density*, and whether it correlates with an identifiable origin vertex/region rather than merely with generation depth;
6. if all computational possibilities are represented, what measure/selection rule yields the observed subset and its stable laws.

These are **test obligations and uncommitted model dimensions**, not six new premises.

## 8. Review conclusion

The researcher's rejection of pre-existing *where* is logically consistent with Premise 1. A **causal beginning without a spatial center** is a coherent candidate. The Ruliad supplies a significant adjacent speculative formalization for many possible rule histories and observer-conditioned observations, not a proof that all are physically executed. Three-dimensional geometry requires identifiable connectivity scaling, not just indefinite branching. **Matter concentration around a spatial root is a model-dependent possibility but not a logical consequence of a single causal source.**

A constructive way to proceed is to test multiple causal-generation rules—including a rooted causal-event model, an initially multiply rooted model and a collection of alternative causal histories—against independently specified geometry and observer-access tests. Do not bake three-dimensionality or mass concentration into the representation before the test.
